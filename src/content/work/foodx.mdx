## The problem

Deciding what to cook is hard when you're a student or new to cooking. You open the fridge, see a few ingredients, and have no idea what to make with them.

FoodX's idea: scan your fridge with AI, identify what's there, and get recipes based on what you already have. You could also pick individual ingredients to see what you could cook.

## My role

I joined FoodX as a UX/UI Design Intern and moved into a full-time role. It was an early-stage startup with a small, close team of about five designers and six to eight developers. I worked with the design team and directly with developers, the CTO and the CFO.

My focus was user journeys, recipe discovery and designing engaging interactions.

## Research

I studied existing cooking and recipe apps to understand their patterns and spot opportunities for FoodX.

## Key decisions

### Make the first experience about your fridge

I worked on recipe discovery and the first-time experience so new users could quickly understand the product and get from "what do I have?" to "what can I cook?".

### Turn the shopping list into a basket

Shopping lists usually rely on simple checkboxes. For an audience of students and new cooks, I wanted something more engaging.

I designed a gamified basket: you add the fruit, vegetables and other ingredients you need into a virtual basket, see your progress as it fills, and earn reward points when you finish the list. The aim was to make a routine task feel interactive and rewarding.

## Outcome

- Designed recipe discovery and the first-time experience around the fridge-scan idea.
- Took the gamified basket from concept to a tested Figma prototype, exploring how visual feedback, progress and rewards can work in an everyday consumer app.
- Grew from intern to a full-time designer on the team.

## What I'd do differently

I'd spend more time validating with the target audience, especially students and people with little cooking experience, through structured usability tests on recipe discovery, onboarding and ingredient selection.

For the basket, I'd test it against a normal shopping list to check whether the extra interaction really improved engagement without making the task harder. And I'd set success measures early, like recipe discovery completion, shopping-list completion and retention.

> Interaction design can make everyday tasks more engaging, but good gamification should always support the user's actual goal, not add complexity for its own sake.
